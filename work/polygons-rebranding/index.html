Rebranding Polygons

Brand identity & visual system · 2021

Polygons makes everyday products that fold flat, starting with a measuring spoon that becomes three sizes depending on how you pick it up. The product took off faster than the brand around it, so before it reached the market, the visual identity needed a second look.

Polygons had brought in a team of graphic designers for this. I worked from inside the company as part of that team, with the founder closely involved in every round. He wanted to see working examples more than a booklet of rules, so the system was shaped by real use cases from the start.

Where it started

Polygons began as a student assignment: take a very simple product and find a better approach to it. Rahul Agarwal picked the measuring spoon, an object most people assume is already perfect, and looked to origami for an answer. A few folds turned a flat sheet into several measures.

The spoon is nearly invisible when not in use and takes shape when it’s needed. Since then, Polygons has won a competition, been re-shared around 100,000 times on Tumblr and raised over a million dollars in crowdfunding.

Why re-brand

Polygons took off so quickly that there was little time to imagine a complete brand. The focus was on making a revolutionary product, and the graphics existed only to support it. Now the brand needed to bring its elements together, feel cohesive and simple while staying versatile, and this time be seen through the eyes of its customers.

“We’re not looking to change the look, we are just looking to streamline.”

For the founder, success meant applications rather than rules: things people would recognise as part of the same family. By the end, there had to be a clear set of internal rules, so any new team member could pick up the system and use it quickly, and a reference document to help the team build assets as Polygons grows.

Reading the Kickstarter campaign

The campaign was the public face of Polygons, so it was the natural starting point. Since the brand hadn’t reached shops yet, few people knew it, which left us free to remodel everything if needed. The job was to find what worked and what didn’t.

  1. 01Lack of consistency

    It opens on a positive note, but darker assets later on give it a gaming feel, and a hand-drawn animation at the end clashes with the serious tone.

  2. 02Product-centred images work

    Photos lead the eye straight to the product, a hand grounds it in reality and the light vignette feels clean. The line overlays hinted at the engineering; could they also point back to origami?

  3. 03The video falls short

    It explains the function well, but the mood turns dark, the excitement is lost and a new green breaks the palette.

Stills from the campaign video: the function is clear, but the mood and the green captions drift from the rest of the brand.

Listening to the client

Rahul had already started polishing the brand himself and wanted us to build on top of it. He walked us through its strengths and weaknesses, and the elements he had already locked in. Three practical rules came out of those conversations.

  1. 01Go minimal

    The product makes the sale, so the focus should stay on it. A minimal system also makes it easier for future products to take on the same skin.

  2. 02Prepare for expansion

    Polygons isn’t a spoon company; more products are on the way. Every piece had to be versatile enough for new items to fit in proudly.

  3. 03Play with extra elements

    Could parts of the identity, not just the logo, tell you something is from Polygons?

The products are used by people of all ages, at first mainly in the USA, Canada and Europe, and priced a little above ordinary ones. That premium is justified by the design and the small moment of magic that comes with it, so we looked at the brand’s personality, rather than demographics, to understand who it speaks to.

Choosing a typeface

The founder had clear criteria: a sans serif, a circular “0” to keep the geometry, sharp apexes on letters like A, M and V, appealing in both upper and lower case, available on Google or Adobe Fonts, and preferably uncommon.

A shortlist of Gill Sans, Transat Text, Brandon Grotesque, Lato, Open Sans and Brother 1816 went to a poll with a wide range of people. Brother 1816 was chosen: geometric shapes with humanist strokes, structured yet free flowing, with enough personality for headlines and branding.

Finding the personality

We used brand archetypes to understand the brand as a person, and through it, the people it would attract. Three stood out.

  1. CreatorRe-invention, intention, imagination

    Highly functional products with moments of delight, and a sense that something new is always being made.

  2. RebelA light-hearted outlaw

    Polygons was born from challenging something everyone thought was already perfect.

  3. ExplorerThe audience

    People who want new, authentic experiences and respond to a sense of freedom and limitlessness.

A creator with a pinch of rebel, for people who are explorers.

Three pointers for the visual language

  1. 01Limitlessness reinvented

    Make people feel they aren’t limited to the status quo: their first glimpse of what the brand is about.

  2. 02Engineered in reality

    However unconventional, show that these products are tried, tested and engineered for real kitchens.

  3. 03Moments of magic

    Small moments of delight that people want to share, and that keep them coming back.

Four approaches

Each round got a little closer to letting the product be the hero.

01

Powerful, but the wrong world

The first round came straight from the product and the first feelings it gave us. It looked striking, but dark, angular and dramatic, it felt more like video games and high-performance devices. A simpler version without the background kept two things everyone liked: the drama, and the soft edge where the product blends into the background.

02

Lighter, closer, a little dull

The second round started from how the product makes people feel: limitless and a bit magical. Lighter tones suited that much better and felt premium, but the way the colours were used made the whole image look dull.

03

The product is the hero

This is where it became clear that the real hero of Polygons will always be its products. The visual system’s job is to show them in a way that inspires awe while lifting the brand. Photographs and renders became the main expressive tool, giving real-world context and highlighting the best features with a sense of curiosity.

04

Grounded, with a twist

Inspired by neumorphism, the fourth approach stayed product-centred but added soft, almost out-of-this-world surfaces, while real-world contexts kept the product grounded. It sat right where the creator, rebel and explorer meet, and became the direction we took forward.

The base

Taking approach 4 forward, we built a hypothetical long-scroll Polygons website from the best of both styles. That gave us a simple visual language to act as a foundation; every later experiment was layered on top of it.

The assets were flexible enough for the upcoming product line to adopt easily. The next step was to give the system more personality without losing that versatility.

01

Lines and the triangle

Lines and dots in the references made images feel sturdy and well engineered, a good way to hint at the engineering behind the products. Polygons doesn’t shout about its technical challenges, though, so the lines had to stay subtle.

The logo is a simple triangle, so we tried it as a pair of bookends marking a boundary. Building it into photographs as well felt forced, and that part was dropped.

02

Can a gradient alone say Polygons?

Colours are often remembered more than anything else about a brand. Gradients create new colours by blending two, and feel modern and fresh. The question was whether a gradient, used as a texture on type, could tell you something is from Polygons with no logo or product in sight.

An earlier designer’s gradient was close but not quite right, so it became my starting point. From there I explored freely, tested the textures on banners, stepped back from 3D shapes that carried a message of their own, and returned to simpler, linear blends.

The final gradient

On its own, as flat patches or a full-bleed fill, it isn’t the most exciting set of colours. On type, which is where it lives, it works. The red sits neither too warm nor too purple, and the range from light pink to dark maroon gives the text a lot of variation in lightness, which creates the hopeful, magical feel.

Next to photos and renders, the red gradient didn’t always sit well. So the gradient started taking its colours from the image itself: the picture extends into the text and blurs, and the type becomes part of the scene. That also opens the door to animations on scroll.

03

Photography and type

Images make the first impression, often before the tagline is read. For Polygons, the mood became soft, clean and minimal, with a touch of realism, because these future-looking products are real and ready to buy.

Most images would have a calm, low-contrast area around the product, which left room for type. One approach placed a bold tagline behind the highlighted product, so the line catches the eye first and the product backs it up.

04

Renders

Polygons sells products, and well-crafted 3D renders show them at their best: every detail composed to highlight the functional design and the physical form. The mood followed the photographs: soft, minimal and clean.

Renders needed no background props, since each highlights a single function. Elements follow real-world physics wherever there’s a real context, and break it only when there’s a reason, so nothing raises questions.

05

More character for the type

Brother 1816 carried the brand well, but small canvases like social posts need information shouted in big letters, mostly numbers: “24 hours left”, “25% off”. Oswald was paired with it as a secondary typeface for those moments.

With a minimal, versatile base in place, the type could take on more personality: numbers interacting with the product, from a safe solid fill, to a slight blur that softens the frame, to 3D frosted figures that make the two worlds merge.

The outcome

A minimal, flexible system the next product can step straight into.

Social media

Posts for launch moments, features and offers, bringing the gradient, renders, photographs and both typefaces together.

Launch teasers: “We’re unfolding”, and the spoon folding from flat.

Website

The base system grown into product pages, feature stories and a timeline of the journey so far, on desktop and mobile.

Print

An invoice, a thank-you mailer and a business card, carrying the gradient and the wordmark.

Along the way

Alongside the identity, I worked on ways to explain the spoon’s features. “3 in 1” mattered most: it’s one spoon that transforms on demand, not three, and it folds back into a stable, very thin flat shape. Fold studies and storyboards explored how to show that, along with ideas for food safe, easy to store, magnetic, two sizes and more.

The spoon would launch in several colours, so I helped choose them from the Pantone range, first digitally and then from the physical book. The hardest part was imagining each colour once textured on silicone and plastic; samples were made and adjusted based on people’s opinions.

What came next, and what I learnt

With a visual language in place, the next step was to test it and gather reviews. A marketing team joined, a brand guideline manual was planned, and work moved on to the website, packaging and promotional material. The photographs in these mock-ups were stand-ins for reference; new shoots were to follow the guidelines.

Working closely with Rahul and the core team taught me a lot beyond graphic design. Polygons also pushed me into 3D. I’d always been comfortable sketching but struggled to make pictorial assets; learning 3D changed how I imagine visual solutions beyond the flat page, and turned my weakest area into one I enjoy.
